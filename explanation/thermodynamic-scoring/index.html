Thermodynamic Scoring¶

BaitBench's thermodynamic simulation mode uses the SantaLucia (1998) nearest-neighbor (NN) model to compute the stability of each probe-target duplex, then converts that stability into a per-fragment capture probability. This page explains the model and how it translates to capture efficiency predictions.

Why Thermodynamics?¶

A probe captures its target if the probe-target duplex is stable enough under the hybridization conditions. Stability is determined by:

  1. Sequence composition: GC base pairs form three hydrogen bonds (stronger) vs AT/AU two bonds (weaker)
  2. Context effects: the stability of each base pair depends on its neighbours (nearest-neighbor effects)
  3. Temperature: higher temperature destabilises duplexes; mismatches are more disruptive at high temperature
  4. Salt concentration: Na⁺ ions screen the negative phosphate backbone charges, stabilising duplexes

A simple GC-content model misses all the context effects. The SantaLucia (1998) model captures them by summing stacking energies for each adjacent base-pair step — the dominant contribution to duplex stability.

The SantaLucia Nearest-Neighbor Model¶

The nearest-neighbor model expresses ΔG of duplex formation as:

ΔG°(T) = ΔH° - T × ΔS°

Where ΔH° and ΔS° are the enthalpy and entropy of hybridization, each computed by summing over all adjacent dinucleotide steps in the sequence:

ΔH° = Σ ΔH°(stacking) + ΔH°(initiation)
ΔS° = Σ ΔS°(stacking) + ΔS°(initiation)

SantaLucia (1998) provides a unified table of 10 unique nearest-neighbor parameters (the 16 possible dinucleotide steps reduce to 10 by symmetry). BaitBench uses these published values directly.

1. Stacking terms¶

For each consecutive base-pair dinucleotide in the probe-target alignment (5'→3'), BaitBench looks up the stacking ΔH° and ΔS° from the SantaLucia (1998) table. Mismatched positions contribute zero stacking energy (they break the consecutive run).

This means a probe with 3 mismatches in a run of otherwise matched positions loses the stacking contributions of all bases adjacent to those mismatches, not just the mismatches themselves. Clustered mismatches are disproportionately destabilising.

2. Initiation terms¶

Two initiation penalties are added, one for the first Watson-Crick pair of the aligned duplex and one for the last. SantaLucia (1998) gives separate AT and GC initiation parameters, and BaitBench applies whichever matches each terminal pair.

Initiation is only charged when at least one stacking step exists. An isolated Watson-Crick pair flanked by mismatches cannot form a stable duplex, so paying its initiation cost would produce ΔG > 0 — implying the probe is actively repelled, which is unphysical.

3. Salt correction (Owczarzy et al. 1997)¶

The SantaLucia parameters are derived at 1 M Na⁺. For realistic hybridization buffers, BaitBench corrects the entropy term for the actual Na⁺ concentration:

ΔS([Na⁺]) = ΔS(1 M) + 0.368 × (n_wc - 1) × ln([Na⁺])

Where n_wc is the number of Watson-Crick paired positions and [Na⁺] is molar. At 1 M, ln(1) = 0 and no correction applies. At lower salt the correction is negative, making ΔG less favourable — matching the physical fact that duplexes are less stable when there are fewer cations to screen the phosphate backbone. As with initiation, the correction is skipped when there is no stacking.

The three contributions are then combined at the hybridization temperature:

ΔG = ΔH°(stacking + initiation) - T × ΔS°(stacking + initiation + salt) / 1000

With T in Kelvin, ΔH° in kcal/mol, ΔS° in cal/(mol·K), and ΔG in kcal/mol. More negative = more stable.

From ΔG to Occupancy¶

ΔG says how stable a duplex is; capture needs a probability. BaitBench converts one to the other with the Langmuir isotherm for reversible binding, P + T ⇌ PT:

K     = [probe] · exp(-ΔG / (R × T))
theta = K / (1 + K)

Where: - R = 1.987 × 10⁻³ kcal/(mol·K) (gas constant) - T = hybridization temperature in Kelvin - ΔG is in kcal/mol - [probe] is the probe concentration, in the arbitrary units absorbed into K

theta is the occupancy: the probability that this probe is bound to this site at equilibrium. Three properties matter, none of which a raw Boltzmann weight exp(-ΔG/RT) has:

  • It is bounded in [0, 1], so it can be used directly as a probability. The raw weight is unbounded — a perfect 120-mer at 65 °C gives ΔG ≈ −92 kcal/mol and exp(-ΔG/RT) ≈ 1e59, which works as a relative weight within one probe's hit list but cannot be a capture probability.
  • It saturates. A strong binder approaches theta = 1 and goes no further, so capture cannot be manufactured for an organism that has little template present.
  • It is concentration-dependent, which is the knob --capture-fraction back-solves.

In the weak-binding limit (K ≪ 1), theta ≈ K ∝ exp(-ΔG/RT), so this generalises Boltzmann weighting rather than discarding it.

Occupancy is evaluated as a logistic in log space, theta = 1/(1 + exp(-ln K)), because K itself overflows a 64-bit float for a well-matched probe.

Earlier versions used exp(-ΔG/RT) clamped to a minimum of 1.0, on the reasoning that probes can enrich fragments but never deplete them. Occupancy needs no such clamp: it is a probability by construction, and an unfavourable ΔG simply puts theta near zero, so a bad probe contributes almost nothing instead of looking neutral.

From Occupancy to Fragment Sampling¶

There are no longer two routes into fragments.fa. Every fragment in the library has a single retention probability:

p_retain(f) = 1 - (1 - p_ns) · prod_i (1 - theta_i)

over the probes i whose alignments overlap fragment f, where p_ns is non-specific carryover (--background-retention).

Read directly: a fragment escapes capture only if every overlapping probe fails to bind it and it is not carried over. A fragment overlapping no probe reduces to p_ns. A fragment overlapping several gets carryover plus each probe's independent chance, so probe density compounds: twenty probes at theta = 0.2 retain a fragment 99% of the time, two retain it 36% of the time.

Affinity and probe density now act on the same quantity, which they did not before. There is no stage at which a probe is chosen uniformly, so a strong probe really does capture more than a weak one.

Why this makes composition emerge¶

p_retain is a property of a fragment, not of the library. So the expected number of fragments retained from a reference is abundance × retention, and the output's composition follows from the input's rather than being imposed on it. Two consequences the previous model could not produce:

  • A dilution series titrates. Halving an organism's abundance halves its reads, because retention is unchanged by what else is in the tube.
  • Per-locus efficiency is separable from abundance, which is what real capture data shows: relative capture between loci of the same organism stays roughly constant across a dilution series spanning orders of magnitude, while absolute yield tracks input.

Sampling without rejection¶

Drawing fragments by accept/reject would be hopeless at realistic enrichments: in a library that is 99.996% off-target at p_ns ≈ 1e-3, roughly a thousand candidates are discarded per keeper.

Instead, note that p_retain is piecewise constant in position — it changes only where some probe's overlap window begins or ends. So for each reference BaitBench decomposes positions into runs of constant p_retain, integrates them exactly into a retention mass M = Σ p_retain, and samples in two stages:

  1. a reference, with probability proportional to weight × M;
  2. a start position within it, proportional to p_retain.

Every draw is a keeper, the cost is proportional to the number of runs rather than the number of attempts, and the overall yield and enrichment fall out analytically instead of being estimated by counting rejections. They are reported in fragments.model_stats.tsv, and per-reference in fragments.retention.tsv.

Because there is no quota split, capture and carryover fragments are interleaved in output order by construction.

What --capture-fraction sets¶

--capture-fraction is a preset over [probe]: of the fragments carrying enough of a probe's target site, the fraction the probes pull down. Capture efficiency is monotone in concentration, so BaitBench inverts it by bisection once, before sampling.

It describes the panel and the protocol — not the output. It is deliberately independent of library composition, because a parameter that targeted the realized on-target fraction would make the solver compensate for added distractor, pinning composition exactly as the old quota did.

Simple Mode vs Thermodynamic Mode¶

In --simulate-mode simple, ΔG is not computed. Every placed probe is assigned the same occupancy, so all alignments are equally likely to capture and both temperature and sequence composition drop out. Probe density still matters — overlapping probes compound as above — so simple mode is a useful null model: it shows what coverage looks like when only "is this position covered, and by how many probes" is in play.

Measured on the minimal example, both targets come out at efficiency ≈ 0.50 in simple mode, against 0.896 and 0.097 in thermodynamic mode. The aggregate is the same, because --capture-fraction pins it; the mode decides how it is distributed across loci.

Feature Thermodynamic Simple
Mismatch penalty Yes — via ΔG No
Temperature effect Yes — --hybridization-temperature affects capture No
GC content effect Yes — high-GC probes capture more efficiently No
Probe-position weighting Occupancy from ΔG One shared occupancy for every probe
Probe density effect Yes Yes — density is independent of ΔG
Use case Realistic efficiency prediction Coverage testing, speed

Thermodynamic mode is more accurate; simple mode is faster and useful for understanding which positions probes cover at all, independent of efficiency.
